Part 2: Is Social Norms Research “Not Communication Enough”?

The Critique and Four Dimensions of Communication

The critique has been voiced by reviewers and editors, and Rimal and Lapinski (2015) themselves acknowledged that the idea that members come to understand, negotiate, and accept or reject norms through communication had not received adequate attention (see also Yanovitzky & Rimal, 2006). To the extent that the critique presumes that research is communication research only when communication among group members is directly observed, the presumption is too narrow. Norms-based interventions rest on “a central assumption about the role of communication” (Lapinski & Rimal, 2005, p. 137). Thus, a study that theorizes and manipulates features of a normative message and analyzes how recipients perceive it examines communication effects, whether or not it observes conversation. This essay therefore considers four dimensions along which communication can be centered: as a channel that provides normative information, as a process through which a norm is interpreted, as a condition of normative influence, and as a process through which collective norms are reproduced or changed. The dimensions build on Lapinski and Rimal’s (2005) argument that communication both forms perceptions about norms and acts as “a conduit of influence” (p. 127).

How Communication Processes Are Centered

With respect to the first dimension, interpersonal communication was measured at the start of this line of work. Rimal and Real (2003) treated talk about drinking with friends or siblings as a central concept and found it strongly associated with consumption. Rather than treating the association as causal, they proposed that “communication among like-minded individuals is a principal mechanism through which norms are transmitted” (p. 199). Mediated channels have also been studied, as formative research for campaigns (Smith et al., 2006) and experiments with norm appeals (Lapinski et al., 2007, 2013; Liu & Lapinski, 2024, 2025) treat the message as the channel through which normative information reaches an audience, and this channel moves perceived norms reliably (Rhodes et al., 2020).

The second dimension concerns interpretation, that is, how recipients construe the prevalence, approval, and sanctions that a normative message conveys. Lapinski et al. (2013) found that participants perceived the prevalence conveyed by each hand washing poster as intended but rated the high-prevalence statistic as less believable than the low-prevalence one. Liu and Lapinski (2025) found that stating sanctions explicitly strengthened an injunctive appeal only among participants who preferred low-context communication. These studies measured interpretation as an outcome of exposure rather than observing how it is formed. Rimal and Lapinski (2015) called for assessing “the manipulated norms, as understood by participants” (p. 404); how that understanding is reached remains to be examined.

The third dimension treats communication as a condition of normative influence. The TNSB proposes that injunctive norms, outcome expectations, and group identity moderate the relationship between descriptive norms and behavior (Rimal & Real, 2005), and Lapinski and Rimal (2005) added behavioral privacy. Privacy is a communicative condition: when a behavior cannot be known to others “either by observation or by communication about the behavior,” neither prevalence information nor sanctions can circulate (Lapinski & Rimal, 2005, p. 141). In the field, the data were not consistent with the predicted interaction between normative messages and privacy (Lapinski et al., 2013). Chung and Rimal (2016) placed interpersonal discussion and media exposure among the contextual moderators of normative influence, noting that descriptive norms predicted drinking more strongly among students who had discussed alcohol (Real & Rimal, 2007).

The fourth dimension is where the critique has the most purchase. Collective norms are defined as emerging through interaction, and Rimal and Lapinski (2015) proposed that they drive perceived descriptive norms, “further enhanced through communication processes” (p. 397). Yet the formation and change of norms within a group have rarely been measured.[4] The gap lies less in theorizing than in testing. Communication has been theorized as the means by which norms are formed and transmitted (Lapinski & Rimal, 2005), but the TNSB does not address “the ways in which normative information is communicated” (Lapinski & Rimal, 2005, p. 133), and measurement has followed the model.

Unique Contributions of Communication Science

Given this account, the contributions of communication science are not limited to methods and message design. First, the field has supplied conceptual explication on which much current work relies, including the distinction between collective and perceived norms, the specification of behavioral attributes such as privacy and ambiguity, and the clarification of how descriptive, injunctive, and subjective norms differ (Lapinski & Rimal, 2005; Park & Smith, 2007; Rimal & Lapinski, 2015). Second, it has theorized the relational variables through which norms operate: the referent group, identification with that group, and communication among its members (Rimal & Real, 2003, 2005). Third, it has treated recipients’ perception of the message as data. Experiments in this tradition have checked whether recipients perceived the intended norm, believed it, and inferred sanctions (Lapinski et al., 2007, 2013; Liu & Lapinski, 2025), whereas in Cialdini et al.’s (1990) field experiments “whether normative perceptions or some other variable was manipulated was not clear” (Lapinski et al., 2013, p. 23). Fourth, it has treated culture as a feature of communication, as when preferences for implicit versus explicit communication conditioned the effects of injunctive appeals that stated or implied sanctions (Liu & Lapinski, 2025).

Gaps in the Literature

The gaps can be stated in terms of the four dimensions. With respect to the channel, peer communication has been measured in cross-sectional surveys and as frequency rather than content, so that peer selection and reverse causation (Rimal & Real, 2003) cannot be ruled out. With respect to interpretation, an injunctive appeal without stated sanctions raised perceived sanctions (Liu & Lapinski, 2025), but how recipients infer sanctions, and whether it matters who would impose them (Douglas et al., 2024), remain unexamined. With respect to change, Rimal and Lapinski (2015) found no evidence that interventions had changed injunctive or collective norms (p. 404). During the COVID-19 pandemic, the norm–behavior relationships were weaker, not stronger, at higher levels of outcome expectations and group orientation (Yoon et al., 2024), which suggests that the moderating propositions of the TNSB may not hold while norms are in flux. With respect to culture, about half of the studies in one content analysis were conducted in the United States (Shulman et al., 2017), and cultural processes have been proposed more often than tested directly.

Three Research Ideas

The first idea addresses the interpersonal channel with a three-wave panel of first-year students in residence halls, beginning at orientation, before students have joined their new social networks (Rimal & Real, 2005). At each wave, participants would report their talk about drinking by partner, frequency, and content (i.e., how much others drink vs. whether others approve), perceived norms for referents matched to those partners, and their own drinking. Floor-level averages of drinking would index collective norms (Rimal & Lapinski, 2015), and random-intercept cross-lagged models would separate within-person change from stable differences (Hamaker et al., 2015). It is predicted that talk about how much peers drink will be positively associated with subsequent perceived descriptive norms and that talk will strengthen the association between collective and perceived descriptive norms. A panel clarifies temporal order rather than causation.

The second idea addresses interpretation with an online experiment that crosses sanction (implied vs. explicit) with source (fellow members of the referent group vs. the larger community) in an injunctive norm appeal, with a message-without-norms control. Sanctions may come from peers or from “the larger group to which this student belongs” (Rimal & Real, 2003, p. 187), and pressure to conform is greater when norms come from close rather than distal others because defiance is more likely to become known to them (Chung & Rimal, 2016). To measure rather than assume their inference, recipients would first describe in an open-ended item what would follow a violation and from whom, and then rate the likelihood of specific sanctions from each source. It is predicted that explicit sanctions will increase perceived sanctions relative to implied sanctions, especially when attributed to fellow members of the referent group. Because explicitness distinguishes low- from high-context messages (Hall, 1976), the design manipulates a cultural feature of the message. The same messages could then be presented in countries that vary in context preference, with back-translation and invariance testing (e.g., Liu & Lapinski, 2024), to test whether the inference of implied sanctions is the process through which culture alters injunctive appeals.

The third idea addresses interpretation as a process by delivering normative feedback as a conversation. Personalized normative feedback reduces drinking modestly, in part by correcting perceived norms (Neighbors et al., 2010), but its scalable forms transmit information in one direction, so that the interpersonal discussion through which misperceptions are thought to arise (Lapinski & Rimal, 2005) is absent from their correction. Chatbots based on large language models make a two-way exchange feasible, and such dialogues have durably reduced conspiracy beliefs (Costello et al., 2024). First-year students who drink heavily and overestimate peer drinking would receive identical normative statistics through a static report, an informational chatbot, or a relational chatbot that adds empathy and self-disclosure, with the AI source label held constant. The primary outcome would be retention of the correction in perceived descriptive norms at a 2-week follow-up, and conversation logs would be coded for the objections recipients raise and the explanations they accept, so that interpretation is observed rather than inferred.

Conclusion

The critique that social norms research is not communication enough does not fit an account that has located norms in communication from its first formulation. Communication has been centered as a channel, a matter of interpretation, and a condition of normative influence, but least as the process through which norms form and change. Culture has been treated more often as a sample characteristic than as a process. The three ideas are designed to measure these processes rather than assume them.
