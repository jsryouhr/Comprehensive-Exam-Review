Theories of social norms were developed largely with U.S. and Western European samples, and none directly accounts for cultural dynamics in normative influence (Lapinski et al., 2022). Research on cultural dynamics has studied co-cultural groups within national boundaries and compared countries (Liu et al., 2022). In this essay, the term refers both to the variation in normative influence that such studies document and to the processes that produce it. Culture may shape how norms are conceptualized, how important they are relative to other influences, and the role of psychosocial dynamics in their effects (Lapinski et al., 2022). Research has located culture in the nation, in the individual, and, least often, in the group whose norms are studied.
[A]
First, culture has been located in the nation. Commonly, nationality serves as a proxy, and differences between national samples are attributed to culture after the fact. The same norm appeals shifted the targeted perceptions more in a Chinese than in a U.S. student sample, a difference the authors related to tightness, institutional signals, and message interpretation (Liu & Lapinski, 2024). None of these was measured, and the samples also differed in survey mode. Alternatively, a dimension is scored at the level of the nation, as in Gelfand et al.'s (2011) tightness–looseness scores for 33 nations. Both approaches locate culture at the level of the nation, whereas country and culture are incongruent under most conditions because co-cultural groups share national identities (Liu et al., 2022).
[B]
Second, in response to the criticism that such work makes broad generalizations about people from different countries, researchers have measured cultural orientation in individuals (Lapinski et al., 2007; Liu & Lapinski, 2024). Group orientation, the extent to which group goals prevail over individual goals, is distinct from group identity, the TNSB moderator that refers to similarity with and aspiration toward a specific referent group (Lapinski et al., 2007). Group-oriented participants held positive water conservation attitudes and intentions regardless of perceived prevalence, which was negatively associated with both among the individualistically oriented (Lapinski et al., 2007). The relationships between perceived norms and physical distancing were steeper at lower levels of group orientation (Yoon et al., 2024), and group orientation predicted intentions to prevent food waste directly without moderating the effects of norm appeals (Liu & Lapinski, 2024). For behaviors that benefit the collective, then, group orientation may function more as a direct motive than as an amplifier of normative information. Liu and Lapinski (2025) measured two further orientations in Chinese and U.S. adults, both of which differed by country in the expected direction. Stating sanctions explicitly in an injunctive appeal raised perceived injunctive norms and intentions, relative to the same appeal without them, among people who preferred low-context communication but not among those who preferred high-context communication. Higher perceived cultural tightness strengthened the effect of an injunctive appeal on intentions but weakened the effect of an appeal with explicit sanctions on perceived norms, which the authors suggested may reflect sanctions already being anticipated in tighter cultures (Liu & Lapinski, 2025).
[C]
Third, culture has been treated as the context that defines what a norm is. Liu et al. (2022) proposed a model for culturally contextualized measurement in which concepts and items are derived from interviews within a cultural group before they are tested, and applied it with ethnically Tibetan pastoralists in Western China. In interviews, herding decisions were constrained by ecological, market, and policy forces, explicit communication about norms arose mainly when problems or policy changes occurred, and sanctions for deviation were implied rather than stated (Lapinski et al., 2022). Injunctive norms were therefore defined without sanctions (Liu et al., 2022), a departure from definitions that make sanctions the feature that separates injunctive from descriptive norms (Lapinski & Rimal, 2005).
[D]
Across these approaches, the processes through which culture is expected to alter normative influence have been proposed more often than measured. Rimal and Lapinski (2015) pointed to motivations to comply, and Liu and Lapinski (2025) reasoned that people who prefer high-context communication infer sanctions that a message leaves unstated. Liu and Lapinski measured perceived sanctions, but they tested high- versus low-context preference as a moderator of perceived injunctive norms as a whole rather than of that inference. None of the studies reviewed here manipulated culture.
